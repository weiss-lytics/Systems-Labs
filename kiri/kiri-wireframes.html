Why capture comes before search
Most note apps open on a search bar or a list — both assume you already know what you're looking for. But the person opening Kiri usually just had a thought, not a question. Leading with "Plant an Idea" removes the decision of where to put it.
tap to collapse
Greeting sets a gentle tone
"What are we dreaming up today?" reframes the blank page as an invitation, not a task. The "we" matters too — it implies Kiri is a thinking partner, not a filing cabinet.
tap to collapse
Recent Sparks, not "all notes"
Calling saved ideas "Sparks" instead of "Notes" keeps language consistent with the garden metaphor — every spark is a seed that may or may not grow into something bigger.

The core rule of this screen
Users should never feel pressured to organize ideas before capturing them. No required fields, no category picker, no title prompt. Just a cursor and room to think.
tap to collapse
Quick actions are optional, not gates
Voice, Screenshot, Link, and Code sit below the text — available, never demanded. They exist for the moments thinking happens outside of words.
tap to collapse
Friction is the enemy here
Every extra tap between "I have a thought" and "it's written down" is a chance to lose the thought entirely. This screen is deliberately the simplest in the whole product.

Structure — shape without effort

The raw dump is gently sorted into recognizable sections. The user reviews and refines rather than building from scratch.

Progressive refinement, not full structure
Asking someone to fully document an idea the moment they've had it is asking too much, too soon. Structure reveals shape gradually — five gentle categories instead of a blank documentation template.
tap to collapse
Why this reduces cognitive load
Recognition is easier than recall. Seeing "Challenges" as a labeled section invites a thought, rather than requiring the user to first remember that challenges are worth writing down at all.
tap to collapse
Sections can stay empty
Not every idea has a "Challenges" section yet — and that's fine. An empty section is information too; it shows the user what they haven't thought through.

Screen 04 / 06

Snapshot — for future-you

A compressed summary card — the version of the idea you'd want to see six months from now, with zero context loaded in memory.

Designed for someone with no memory of this
The Snapshot's entire job is helping future-you understand the idea in seconds. Not minutes — seconds. That constraint shaped every line of copy on this card.
tap to collapse
Five fields, no more
Purpose, Core Features, Tech Stack, Value, Future Plans. Each one answers a single question a returning user would ask first. Anything beyond five fields starts to feel like the documentation Kiri was built to avoid.
tap to collapse
A snapshot, not a blueprint
This is intentionally text-first and compact — the visual diagram lives one step later, in Blueprint. Snapshot is for reading quickly; Blueprint is for seeing relationships.

Blueprint — the system made visible

Where Snapshot answers "what is this," Blueprint answers "how does it move." A relationship map of the idea's own lifecycle, rendered like a technical drawing.

01 — Linear, not branching
Every idea moves the same direction through the same four states. Predictability builds trust — the user always knows where they are in the lifecycle.
02 — Structure feeds both children
Snapshot is generated from Structure, not from the raw Capture. Quality compounds — a well-organized idea produces a clearer summary.
03 — Archive is a loop, not an ending
Old ideas in the Archive can be reopened and re-captured. Nothing is ever truly finished — only resting.

Why linear over modular
Every layer has exactly one upstream and one downstream dependency. This keeps the system easy to reason about — and easy to rebuild piece by piece as the product matures.
tap to collapse
AI Processing is a layer, not a feature
Treating intelligence as infrastructure — not a bolt-on chat box — keeps the product's actual value (structure, clarity) separate from whichever model powers it underneath.
tap to collapse
Archive feeds back to User
Though drawn as a line, this system is conceptually a loop — the Archive is what the User opens next. Systems thinking means designing for the second visit, not just the first.
